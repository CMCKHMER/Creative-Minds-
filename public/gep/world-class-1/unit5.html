Grammar
Adverbial Clauses
Time Clauses
Use when, while, before, after, until, as soon as to say when something happens.
When the storm began, the team stopped climbing.
They waited until the wind died down.
As soon as they saw the summit, they cheered.
Reason Clauses
Use because, since, or as to explain why something happened.
The climbers turned back because the weather was dangerous.
Since oxygen levels were low, they moved slowly.
Contrast Clauses
Use although, even though, while, or whereas to show an unexpected difference.
Although it was freezing, they continued climbing.
Even though he was exhausted, he did not give up.
Condition Clauses
Use if, unless, or provided that to talk about possible situations.
If you feel dizzy at altitude, stop and rest.
Unless we leave now, we will be caught in the storm.
Result Clauses
Use so + adjective/adverb + that or such + noun + that.
It was so cold that their water bottles froze.
It was such a dangerous climb that only experts attempted it.
Important: When an adverbial clause comes at the beginning of the sentence, use a comma after it.
Because the weather was bad, they stopped. (comma needed)
They stopped because the weather was bad. (no comma needed)

Reading
Everest's Deadliest Day

🏔️ Everest's Deadliest Day

On May 10, 1996, two teams of climbers stood near the summit of Mount Everest, the highest mountain on Earth. One team was led by Rob Hall, a famous guide from New Zealand. The other was led by Scott Fischer, an experienced American climber. Both teams had paid clients—people who had spent up to $65,000 to reach the top of the world. Because both teams wanted to reach the summit on the same day, the route became crowded.

Although the climbers knew that turning back before 2:00 p.m. was the safe rule, many continued upward. By the time they reached the summit, it was late. Some climbers did not start descending until after 3:00 p.m. Since the weather can change very quickly at high altitude, this delay was extremely dangerous.

While the climbers were still near the summit, a sudden blizzard hit the mountain. The wind was so strong that it knocked people off their feet. The temperature dropped to -40°C. Because visibility was almost zero, several climbers became lost. They could not find the fixed ropes that would guide them down.

Rob Hall stayed behind to help a struggling client, Doug Hansen. Although Hall was an expert, he could not persuade Hansen to move faster. They ran out of oxygen. Since the storm was getting worse, the other guides could not reach them. Hall used his satellite phone to call his wife in New Zealand. "Sleep well, my love," he said. It was the last time anyone heard his voice.

Scott Fischer was also in trouble. He had been feeling sick all day, but he did not want to disappoint his clients. By the time his team found him, he was too weak to walk. Although a guide named Anatoli Boukreev tried to help him, the conditions were too severe. Fischer died on the mountain that night.

In total, eight people died on Everest during that storm. It was the deadliest day in the mountain's history until 2014. Since then, climbers and guides have changed their rules. If the turnaround time is missed, they must go back—no exceptions. Because one delay can cost many lives, the lesson of 1996 is still remembered today.

Writing
A Set of Instructions
Writing Strategy — Explaining the Steps in a Process: When you write instructions, order is everything. Use sequence words (first, next, then, after that, finally) and imperative verbs (check, put, turn, wait). Include warnings or tips so the reader avoids mistakes.

Model Instructions: How to Survive If You Get Lost Hiking

1
Stop and stay calm
Do not panic. Sit down, drink water, and breathe. Panic makes you move faster—and often in the wrong direction.
2
Check your supplies
Look at what you have: water, food, warm clothing, phone, map, whistle. If you have a phone, try to send your location before the battery dies.
3
Decide whether to stay or move
If you told someone your route, stay where you are. If you are near a dangerous area (cliff, river, cold exposure), move to a safer spot and mark your path with rocks or branches.
4
Signal for help
Use a whistle (three blasts is the universal signal for help). Make a large X on the ground with bright clothing or rocks so aircraft can see you.
5
Conserve energy and stay warm
Build a shelter before dark. Keep dry, because wet clothing loses heat 25 times faster than dry clothing. Ration your water, but do not ration food completely—your body needs energy to stay warm.

Speaking
Discuss a Difficult Environmental Issue
Speaking Strategy — Reaching a Compromise: When two sides disagree, a compromise means each side gives up something to reach an agreement. Use phrases like I understand your point, but... and What if we tried...? instead of simply saying no.

The Issue: Tourism on Everest

Every year, hundreds of climbers try to reach the summit of Everest. This creates problems:

  • Trash and abandoned equipment pollute the mountain.
  • Experienced Sherpas take extreme risks to help tourists.
  • Dead bodies on the route cannot be removed safely.
  • Wealthy tourists pay large fees, which helps the local economy.
Position A: Environmental Group

You believe the number of climbers should be limited to 100 per year. You want stricter rules about equipment and waste. You believe tourism money is not worth the environmental damage.

Position B: Local Tourism Board

You believe Everest climbing brings millions of dollars to Nepal. Many local families depend on this income. You want to increase permits to 500 per year. You believe the economy is more important than environmental concerns.

Compromise Phrases

Understanding the other side: "I understand that..." / "I see your point about..." / "You make a valid argument regarding..."

Suggesting a middle ground: "What if we..." / "Would you be willing to..." / "Perhaps we could..."

Agreeing conditionally: "I can accept that, provided that..." / "I'm willing to compromise if..."
