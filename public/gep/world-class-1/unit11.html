Grammar
Phrasal Verbs
What Are Phrasal Verbs?
A phrasal verb is a verb + a small word (preposition or adverb). Together, they have a special meaning that is different from the verb alone.
look = to use your eyes
look up = to search for information
look after = to take care of someone
Weather Phrasal Verbs
clear up = the weather becomes sunny and nice
die down = the wind or storm becomes weaker
hold off = bad weather does not come yet
set in = bad weather begins and continues
warm up = the temperature rises
I hope the rain clears up before the picnic.
The wind died down around midnight.
Let's start the game before the storm sets in.
The weather warmed up suddenly in March.
Separable vs. Inseparable Phrasal Verbs
Separable: You can put the object between the verb and the particle.
Inseparable: The object must come after the particle.
Separable: Turn off the lights. / Turn the lights off.
Inseparable: Look after your sister. (NOT: Look your sister after.)
Inseparable: Get on the bus. (NOT: Get the bus on.)
Tip: If the object is a pronoun (it, him, her, them), it must go between the verb and the particle in separable phrasal verbs.

✅ Turn it off. (NOT: Turn off it.)
✅ Pick her up at 6. (NOT: Pick up her at 6.)
Pronunciation: Stress in Phrasal Verbs

In phrasal verbs, the main stress is usually on the particle (the small word).

"Please turn OFF the lights."
"The storm DIED down around midnight."

Reading
Storm Chasers

🌀 Storm Chasers: Chasing the World's Most Dangerous Weather

Every spring, a strange group of people drive toward danger instead of away from it. They are storm chasers — scientists, photographers, and adventurers who follow tornadoes across the American Midwest. Their goal is to study these violent storms up close, capture incredible images, and better understand how tornadoes form.

Tornadoes are among nature's most destructive forces. A single tornado can destroy an entire neighborhood in minutes. Winds inside a tornado can reach 480 kilometers per hour — faster than most racing cars. The United States experiences more tornadoes than any other country, with over 1,000 reported each year. Most occur in a region known as "Tornado Alley," which includes parts of Texas, Oklahoma, Kansas, and Nebraska.

Dr. Emily Carter has been chasing storms for fifteen years. "People think we're crazy," she says, "but the data we collect saves lives. The more we understand about how tornadoes develop, the better we can predict them and warn communities." Her team uses advanced radar, drones, and special vehicles designed to withstand extreme winds.

Storm chasing is not just about science. It is also about respect. "You cannot control a tornado," Dr. Carter explains. "You have to read the sky, trust your instincts, and know when to stop. The storm is always in charge." Even experienced chasers have close calls. In 2013, a massive tornado in Oklahoma killed three experienced storm chasers and changed how the community approaches safety.

Today, storm chasing is safer but still risky. Teams now share real-time data and use GPS tracking to avoid dangerous situations. Drones can fly into storms that are too dangerous for humans. But for Dr. Carter, there is nothing like seeing a tornado form with her own eyes. "It is terrifying and beautiful at the same time," she says. "It reminds you how powerful nature really is."

Writing
Describe a Time Weather Impacted Your Life
Writing Strategy — Include Figurative Language:
Figurative language makes your writing more interesting and emotional. Instead of just describing facts, use comparisons and creative expressions.

Simile: comparing two things using "like" or "as"
→ "The wind screamed like a train passing through a tunnel."

Metaphor: saying something is something else
→ "The sky was an ocean of dark, angry waves."

Personification: giving human qualities to non-human things
→ "The thunder roared and the lightning danced across the sky."

Model Paragraph: The Day the Storm Changed Everything

Last summer, a typhoon hit my city and changed my perspective on nature forever. The morning started calmly, but by afternoon the sky was a wall of black ink rolling toward us. The wind howled like a wolf outside my window, and rain pounded against the glass as if it wanted to break in. My family and I huddled in the living room, listening to the news and hoping the power would stay on. When the storm finally died down, our street was flooded and trees were lying across the road like fallen soldiers. We spent the next three days cleaning up. It was exhausting, but it also brought our neighborhood together. Everyone helped each other, and I realized that even the worst weather cannot break human connection.

Exercise B — Write your own paragraph

Describe a time when weather had a big impact on your life. Write 120–150 words. Include at least one simile, one metaphor, or one example of personification.

Questions to help you:

Speaking
Which Weather Story Is False?
Exercise A — The Game

Work in groups of four. Each person prepares a 1-minute story about an experience with extreme weather. Three stories should be true. One story should be completely made up. After everyone tells their story, the group votes on which one is false. The person who fools the most people wins!

Storyteller Tips:
• Include specific details (dates, places, names).
• Show emotion in your voice and face.
• If your story is false, mix real details with made-up ones.
• Answer questions confidently.
Listener Tips:
• Listen for details that do not make sense.
• Ask follow-up questions.
• Watch the storyteller's body language.
• Trust your instincts!
Useful Expressions for the Game:

Telling a story: "This happened when I was..." / "I will never forget the day..." / "At first, everything seemed normal, but then..."
Asking questions: "What did you do next?" / "Who was with you?" / "How did you feel at that moment?"
Making guesses: "I think your story is true because..." / "I'm not sure. The part about... seems strange."
Example Story (True)

"Three years ago, I was visiting my grandmother in a small village. One night, the temperature dropped to minus 15 degrees — the coldest winter in fifty years. The pipes in her house froze, so we had no water for three days. We melted snow to flush the toilet and cooked on a small camping stove. It was uncomfortable, but we played cards by candlelight and laughed a lot. Now, every time it snows, I remember that week and smile."
