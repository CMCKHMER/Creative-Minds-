Grammar
Review of Passive Voice
Forming the Passive Voice
Use be (in the correct tense) + past participle.
Active: Scientists discovered a new species.
Passive: A new species was discovered by scientists.
Passive in Different Tenses
Simple Present: am / is / are + past participle
Elephants are protected by law in many countries.
Simple Past: was / were + past participle
The baby elephant was rescued from a well last year.
Present Perfect: has / have been + past participle
Three new species have been identified this decade.
Future: will be + past participle
The injured bird will be released back into the wild next month.
When to Use the Passive Voice
1. When the person doing the action is unknown or unimportant.
The forest was destroyed by fire. (We don't know who started it.)
2. When the action itself is more important than who did it.
Over 100 animals have been saved this year.
3. In formal or scientific writing.
The experiment was conducted over six months.
Important: Only transitive verbs (verbs that take an object) can be used in the passive voice.

✅ The tiger was seen in the jungle. (transitive: see something)
❌ The tiger was slept in the cave. (intransitive: sleep does not take an object)
Pronunciation: The "-ed" Ending in Past Participles

/t/ after voiceless sounds: protected, walked, helped
/d/ after voiced sounds: saved, called, rescued
/ɪd/ after /t/ or /d/ sounds: wanted, needed, decided

Reading
Orphan Elephants

🐘 Orphan Elephants: A Second Chance at Life

In the dusty plains of Kenya, a small team of conservationists runs one of the most remarkable animal rescue centers in the world. The David Sheldrick Wildlife Trust has saved, rehabilitated, and reintroduced over 300 orphaned elephants into the wild since it opened in 1977. Each elephant has a story — and each one is a miracle.

The orphans arrive for many reasons. Some have been found next to their mothers, who were killed by poachers for their ivory. Others have fallen into wells or been separated from their herds during droughts. Many are just days old, still dependent on milk. Without human help, they would die within hours.

At the center, each baby elephant is assigned a team of keepers who stay with it around the clock. The keepers even sleep in the same room as the elephants, because the babies need constant companionship. "Elephants are deeply emotional animals," says head keeper Edwin Lusichi. "When an elephant loses its mother, it experiences grief just like a human child would. Without love and attention, it will stop eating and die of a broken heart."

The center has developed a special milk formula that mimics elephant mother's milk, which is high in fat and protein. Baby elephants are bottle-fed every three hours, day and night. As they grow, they are gradually introduced to vegetation and taught how to find water, dig for minerals, and socialize with other elephants.

When an orphan reaches about three years old, it is moved to a reintegration unit in a protected area. There, it meets older orphans and eventually wild herds. The transition is slow and carefully monitored. Some elephants take years to fully adapt to life in the wild. Others never leave — they stay near the center, visiting their former keepers and bringing their own calves to meet the people who saved them.

The work is expensive and exhausting, but the results speak for themselves. Over 300 elephants have been successfully reintroduced. Many have had their own babies in the wild, creating new generations of elephants that might not have existed without the trust's intervention. "Every elephant we save is a victory against extinction," says Lusichi. "But the real battle is stopping the poaching that creates orphans in the first place."

Writing
Write an Informational Brochure
Writing Strategy — Getting the Reader's Attention:
1. Headline: Use a bold, interesting title that makes people want to read more.
2. Hook: Start with a surprising fact, a question, or a short story.
3. Clear sections: Use headings, bullet points, and short paragraphs.
4. Visuals: Describe what images or icons would go where.
5. Call to action: Tell the reader what they can do to help.

🐢 SAVE THE SEA TURTLES

Did you know? Six of the seven species of sea turtles are endangered. Every year, thousands of baby turtles die before they reach the ocean.

Why are they in danger?

  • Plastic pollution in the ocean is mistaken for food.
  • Beach development destroys nesting sites.
  • Climate change is warming the sand, which affects the gender of hatchlings.
  • Poachers hunt turtles for their shells and eggs.

What is being done? Conservation groups patrol beaches at night to protect nests. Hatchlings are guided safely to the water. Fishing nets are being redesigned to let turtles escape.

What YOU can do:

  • Reduce plastic use — especially single-use bags and straws.
  • Support organizations that protect sea turtle habitats.
  • If you visit a nesting beach, follow the rules and keep your distance.

🌊 Every turtle counts. Act now before it is too late.

Speaking
Debate: What to Do About Three Animals at Risk
Exercise A — Prepare Your Argument

Your class has $50,000 to donate to one animal conservation project. Read about the three options and prepare your argument.

🐅 Option 1: Bengal Tigers in India
Only 3,000 remain. Habitat loss and poaching are the main threats. $50,000 would fund anti-poaching patrols for one year.
🦏 Option 2: Black Rhinos in Namibia
Fewer than 6,000 left. Their horns are worth more than gold on the black market. $50,000 would relocate 10 rhinos to a safer reserve.
🐼 Option 3: Giant Pandas in China
About 1,800 in the wild. Bamboo forests are shrinking due to climate change. $50,000 would plant 5,000 bamboo plants and restore habitat.
1. Which animal deserves the funding most?
2. Is it better to protect a popular animal (like pandas) or a less-known one?
Speaking Strategy — Debating and Defending Your Position:

Opening your argument: "I believe we should fund [animal] because..." / "The most important factor is..."
Giving evidence: "According to the information, only... remain." / "The data shows that..."
Countering the other side: "I understand that [other animal] is also important, but..." / "While that is true, we must consider..."
Closing strongly: "For these reasons, I strongly recommend..." / "The choice is clear: we must..."
Roleplay: The Class Debate

Student A (Tigers): Argue that tigers are apex predators and essential for ecosystem balance. Without them, prey populations explode and destroy vegetation. Use the passive voice: "Their habitat is being destroyed by deforestation."

Student B (Rhinos): Argue that rhinos are more critically endangered and that relocating them has been proven to work. Use statistics and comparatives.

Student C (Pandas): Argue that pandas attract global attention and funding, which helps entire ecosystems. Bamboo forests are protected because of panda conservation. Use cause and effect.
